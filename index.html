Supreme Court of N.S.W.

Tutty v Buckley & Ors

Officers of the League and the Club, sued as representatives (362)

1.A DECLARATION that rules 26, 27, part of 28, 30 and 105(g) are void as unreasonable restraints of trade.
2.An INJUNCTION restraining enforcement of the said rules against the plaintiff.
Granted — Sup. Ct. NSWAppeal → High Court

1. Can the Court intervene?

Tutty argued

Whether the rules were contractual is irrelevant. Restraint of trade is a rule of public policy; instead, it turns on a rule’s practical operation (360–1).

The League: “There is no contract between Tutty and the League. There is nothing for a court to strike down.”Restraint of trade turns on how a rule operates in practice, not on whether it could be enforced in court. The doctrine applies to restraints “howsoever imposed”.375–6; Dickson v Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain [1970] AC 403 applied.

2. Was Tutty’s trade restrained?

Tutty argued

A professional footballer is engaged in trade, and the rules restrained it by preventing him from accepting the better terms other clubs would offer (358, 360–1; Walker v Crystal Palace Football Club Ltd [1910] 1 KB 87 cited).

The League: “This is sport, not trade.”“It may be sport to the amateur, but to a man who is paid for it and makes his living thereby it is his work.” The doctrine applies to employment generally — part-time included.371–2; Walker v Crystal Palace Football Club Ltd [1910] 1 KB 87 applied; Hepworth Manufacturing Co Ltd v Ryott [1920] 1 Ch 1 applied.
The League: “He can simply resign.”“Not to the point.” He may labour, or play cricket — but never again be paid to play league. The choice of resignation is no freedom at all.373; Elford v Buckley (1969) 90 WN (Pt 1) (NSW) 756 overruled.
The League: “We are the only competition/employer in the Rugby League Profession. These are our rules.”The League is not one employer. The clubs are separate employers competing for players, and the rules stop a man from capitalising on that competition.372–3.

3. Were the restraints reasonable?

The test for an unreasonable restraintIs the restraint no wider than reasonably necessary to protect the League’s legitimate interests, and not injurious to the public? The onus is on the League (376–7; Nordenfelt v Maxim Nordenfelt Guns and Ammunition Co Ltd [1894] AC 535 applied; McEllistrim v Ballymacelligott Co-operative Agricultural and Dairy Society Ltd [1919] AC 548 applied).

Tutty argued

Competition can be secured by longer or staggered contracts. A club’s investment is not a protectable interest — “a player’s skill is his property”. The appeal procedure is immaterial (361; Eastham v Newcastle United Football Club Ltd [1964] Ch 413 cited).

The League: “Free movement would wreck the competition.”A legitimate aim — conceded. But it can be secured by contracts of fixed or staggered length; no provisions “so drastic” are needed.377–8.
The League: “He could have appealed, or he could have negotiated a release when he signed.”The player is “completely in the hands of the [appeal] committee” — nothing keeps the restraint inside what a court would call reasonable. Furthermore, a negotiated release assumes advice and bargaining power a young player does not have.379.
The League: “We trained him. The fee repays the club.”The transfer fees were “quite unrelated to any benefit which the player has received”. Replacement cost is “only another way of saying” an employer may bar an employee from leaving unless compensated.378.

4. Relief

Tutty argued

The court’s protection by declaration and injunction is not confined to property rights — it extends to his civil right to work as a footballer (360–2).

The League: “Even if the rules are void, Tutty has no remedy. We never sued him.”The League’s argument here is “mere sophistry.” The League needs no court to enforce its rules, but Tutty needs one to escape them. It is “contrary to the public welfare” that a man be unreasonably prevented from earning his living. Declaration and injunction upheld.380–2; Eastham v Newcastle United Football Club Ltd [1964] Ch 413 approved; Nagle v Feilden [1966] 2 QB 633 approved.

Tigers take it all

Grand Final, S.C.G.BALMAIN 11SOUTH SYDNEY 2

BALMAIN are the premiers of 1969. The Tigers beat South Sydney 11–2 at the Cricket Ground on Saturday, holding the champions tryless — and the premiership comes home to Leichhardt for the first time since 1947.

The premiers absorbed everything the favourites could produce in a bruising opening half and struck when it mattered, turning the contest into one of the great boilovers the competition has seen. The Rabbitohs, champions of the two seasons past and favourites with every bookmaker in town, could not cross the stripe all afternoon.

The Tigers' defensive effort will be talked about for years, rush after rush turned away on the quarter line, and the forwards giving no quarter in the tackle. By the final hooter the Hill was a sea of black and gold, and the premiership was travelling to Leichhardt for the first time in a generation.

Officials of the League declared the season a triumph for the competition and for the game. Celebrations in the inner west are expected to continue for some days, with the team to be received at the Town Hall during the week.

The Rugby League News

Sydney, N.S.W.Monday, 13 December 1971Price 6c

Property of the League?

Balmain’s Dennis Tutty takes the game itself to the High Court — a decision expected today

SYDNEY — The High Court of Australia will today decide whether the rules by which rugby league has governed its players for a decade can stand against the oldest doctrine of the common law of employment.

The retain-and-transfer system, adopted by the New South Wales Rugby Football League in 1959, requires every club to lodge each November a list of the players it retains and a list of those offered for transfer. A retained player may play for his club or for no club at all, and a transfer-listed man may move only upon payment of a fee fixed by his former club in its own discretion.

Mr Tutty, a first-grade player who represented Australia against New Zealand in 1967, refused to play at all throughout the 1969 season after his request for a clearance was refused by the Balmain club. He commenced proceedings in May of that year against the officers of the League and of his club.

Three judges of the Supreme Court of New South Wales declared the rules void as an unreasonable restraint of trade and restrained their enforcement. The League, by special leave, appealed. The Full Bench heard argument on whether a footballer paid for his play is engaged in employment, whether rules of a voluntary association may be restraints of trade at all, and whether the system exceeds what is reasonably necessary to protect the competition.

Officials of the League maintain that without the system the wealthiest clubs would engross the best players and the competition would fail. Supporters of the player reply that a man’s skill is his own, and that no other trade in the Commonwealth permits an employer to hold a man without wages and without work, indefinitely, upon a price of his employer’s choosing.

The first remaining question

What is ‘reasonable’? The Court never says

1

The transfer fee

The training-costs gap.

The Court condemned transfer fees quite unrelated to any benefit the player received but never asked whether a fee capped at genuine training costs could survive. (378)

Easthamwent no further than condemning the fee in combination with retention. The transfer system alone was not so very objectionable. (Eastham v Newcastle United Football Club Ltd [1964] Ch 413)

Butthe gap follows from the facts: Balmain’s fee was discretionary and uncapped, so a calibrated fee was never in issue.

2

The retention fee

The true vice was the absence of any limit.

Retention had no time limit — a club could hold a player it neither played nor paid, however short his service. (378)

Devlynargues that a restraint confined to a player’s first contract might well have survived. The modern AFL draft restrains players only at entry, with free agency after. (Devlyn, ‘Labour Market Controls in the Australian Football League (AFL) and the Common Law Restraint of Trade Doctrine’ (2019) 3 Western Australian Student Law Review 13)

3

The gap was deliberate

It is not for a court to advise in advance what restraints would be reasonable; our function is only to consider whether the rules in their present form impose a greater restraint than is necessary.(377–8)

OutcomePrincipled approach but the League was left guessing until Adamson struck down the NSWRL internal draft on the same reasoning, repeating the same refusal. (Adamson v New South Wales Rugby League Ltd (1991) 31 FCR 242, applying Buckley v Tutty (1971) 125 CLR 353)

The second remaining question

The claim the Court never fully answered

‘A player’s skill is his property and not that of his club’ — Tutty’s claim, 361

Never adopted in name — and for good reason

  • The doctrine protects a liberty, not property. Every authority speaks of the ‘right to work’ and ‘liberty of employment’. (Nagle v Feilden [1966] 2 QB 633; Dickson v Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain [1970] AC 403; Eastham v Newcastle United Football Club Ltd [1964] Ch 413)
  • The term ‘property’ cuts both ways: property can be bought. This is the very logic underpinning a transfer fee.

Adopted in substance

The rules fell because it stopped a player ‘reaping the financial rewards of his own skill’ — reframed in the terms of employment, not ownership. (378)

The result Protection through freedom of employment, not property. This is a framing no club can buy. Quieter, but enough for Tutty to win.
